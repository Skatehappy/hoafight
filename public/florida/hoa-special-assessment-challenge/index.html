HOA Special Assessment Challenge Law in Florida

Florida homeowners associations are governed primarily by Chapter 720 of the Florida Statutes. Under Fla. Stat. § 720.303(2), the board must hold an open meeting to levy any special assessment, and written notice—including a statement that assessments will be considered and the nature of those assessments—must be mailed, delivered, or electronically transmitted to each parcel owner and posted conspicuously on the property at least 14 days before the meeting. Failure to follow this notice rule is one of the most common grounds for challenging a special assessment. The HOA's authority to levy assessments must also be grounded in the declaration of covenants. If the declaration does not authorize the type of expenditure—such as new amenities, capital improvements beyond maintenance, or non-common-area projects—the board cannot impose a special assessment for it without a membership vote, depending on the governing documents. Section 720.308 limits assessments to those authorized in the declaration and consistent with Florida law. Records must be available for inspection under § 720.303(5), so homeowners can demand the budget, meeting minutes, and notice records to verify compliance. If the HOA acted improperly, § 720.305 provides remedies including injunctive relief, damages, and recovery of attorney's fees and costs by the prevailing party. Florida also requires pre-suit mediation for many disputes under § 720.311 before a homeowner can file in court, though monetary collection disputes may proceed differently. Importantly, paying an assessment under protest preserves your right to challenge it while avoiding late fees, interest, and the HOA's powerful lien and foreclosure remedies under § 720.3085.

How a Demand Letter Works in Florida

A Florida HOA special assessment challenge letter works because it forces the board—and its attorney—to confront specific statutory violations in writing, creating a paper trail that can later support litigation or a fee award. Start by identifying the assessment, the date it was approved, and the precise procedural defect: insufficient 14-day notice under § 720.303(2), failure to post notice on the property, lack of authority in the declaration, improper meeting procedure, or absence of a required membership vote. Cite the statute by section number. Request specific records under § 720.303(5), including meeting minutes, the notice mailed to owners, proof of posting, the budget, and the declaration provisions relied upon. Set a reasonable response deadline—typically 14 to 30 days—and state clearly what you want: rescission of the assessment, a corrected vote, a refund, or a payment plan under protest. Reference § 720.305 attorney's fees exposure, which often motivates boards to negotiate rather than litigate. Send the letter by certified mail, return receipt requested, and keep proof of delivery. If the HOA ignores the letter or refuses to cure, you have preserved evidence of bad faith and substantial compliance with pre-suit notice expectations. Many Florida boards quickly correct procedural mistakes once a homeowner demonstrates familiarity with Chapter 720, because the cost of defending a defective assessment plus statutory fees usually exceeds the disputed amount. A clear, statute-grounded demand letter is the single most cost-effective tool a Florida homeowner has.

Procedural Notes for Florida

Florida small claims court (county court) handles disputes up to $8,000, exclusive of interest, costs, and attorney's fees, under Florida Small Claims Rule 7.010. Filing fees range from roughly $55 to $300 depending on the amount. Most HOA disputes that are not pure money collection require pre-suit mediation under Fla. Stat. § 720.311 before filing in circuit court; the demanding party serves a statutory offer to participate, and the responding party has 20 days to reply. The statute of limitations for breach of a written HOA contract is generally five years under § 95.11(2)(b). Always pay disputed assessments under protest in writing to avoid liens and accelerated foreclosure under § 720.3085. Consult a Florida-licensed attorney for case-specific advice.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my Florida HOA levy a special assessment without a homeowner vote?
It depends on your declaration of covenants. Florida law allows boards to levy special assessments if the governing documents authorize it, but many declarations require a membership vote above a certain dollar threshold or for capital improvements. Always read your declaration and bylaws carefully. If the documents require a vote and the board skipped one, the assessment is likely invalid under Fla. Stat. § 720.303 and § 720.308, and you have strong grounds for a written challenge.
How much notice must my HOA give before approving a special assessment?
Under Fla. Stat. § 720.303(2), the HOA must provide at least 14 days written notice to all parcel owners before the board meeting where a special assessment will be considered. The notice must specifically state that assessments will be discussed and describe their nature. The notice must also be posted conspicuously on the property. If your HOA failed to give proper notice, the assessment may be voidable, and a demand letter is your first step.
Should I pay the special assessment while I challenge it?
Generally yes, but pay under written protest. Florida HOAs have powerful collection tools under Fla. Stat. § 720.3085, including liens and foreclosure for unpaid assessments. Refusing to pay risks late fees, interest, attorney's fees, and ultimately your home. Paying under protest preserves your right to seek a refund while avoiding catastrophic collection actions. Mark your check or transmittal letter clearly with 'paid under protest' and reference the dispute.
Can I recover attorney's fees if I successfully challenge the assessment?
Yes. Fla. Stat. § 720.305(1) provides that the prevailing party in an action to enforce or challenge HOA rights and obligations is entitled to recover reasonable attorney's fees and costs. This fee-shifting provision is a powerful tool because it makes HOAs cautious about defending invalid assessments. A demand letter citing § 720.305 often prompts boards to negotiate, since the fee exposure can quickly exceed the assessment amount, especially for smaller disputes.
Do I have to mediate before suing my Florida HOA?
For many non-monetary disputes—such as challenges to assessments based on procedural or governance violations—Fla. Stat. § 720.311 requires pre-suit mediation before filing in circuit court. You serve a statutory demand to participate in mediation, and the HOA has 20 days to respond. Pure debt collection actions for unpaid assessments may proceed directly to court. Sending a demand letter first often resolves the dispute and satisfies good-faith expectations before any formal mediation begins.
