HOA Architectural Approval Denial Law in Georgia

Georgia HOAs derive their authority from recorded covenants, conditions, and restrictions (CC&Rs), and many are also governed by the Georgia Property Owners' Association Act, O.C.G.A. § 44-3-220 through § 44-3-235. The POA Act applies only when a community has affirmatively submitted to it through a recorded declaration; otherwise, the association is governed by general contract law and the common law of restrictive covenants. Either way, Georgia courts have consistently held that architectural review powers must be exercised reasonably and in good faith. In Saunders v. Thorn Woode Partnership and similar cases, Georgia appellate courts have ruled that an HOA's discretion in approving or denying architectural changes is not unlimited—decisions must be tied to standards in the governing documents and applied consistently to similarly situated homeowners. Under O.C.G.A. § 44-5-60, restrictive covenants are strictly construed against the party seeking enforcement, meaning ambiguities generally favor the homeowner. If the declaration does not expressly grant the ARC authority to regulate a specific modification, the HOA likely cannot deny it on that basis. Additionally, the board has fiduciary duties to act in the best interests of the association as a whole, not to single out individual owners. Common grounds for challenging a denial include: (1) the ARC failed to respond within the timeframe required by the covenants, often resulting in automatic approval; (2) the denial was based on subjective aesthetic preferences not codified in written guidelines; (3) the HOA approved similar modifications for other owners; or (4) the ARC failed to provide written reasons as required. Georgia law also recognizes selective enforcement and waiver as defenses when an HOA has historically failed to enforce a restriction.

How a Demand Letter Works in Georgia

A demand letter is often the most cost-effective way to resolve an architectural denial dispute in Georgia. The letter should formally cite the specific provisions of your declaration and bylaws, identify the ARC's procedural or substantive errors, and demand a written response within a stated timeframe—typically 14 to 30 days. Effective Georgia demand letters reference O.C.G.A. § 44-3-223 (where applicable), which requires associations to comply with their own governing documents, and O.C.G.A. § 44-5-60, which mandates strict construction of covenants against the HOA. Your letter should include: a clear description of the modification requested, the date and method of submission, the ARC's response (or lack thereof), evidence of similar approvals granted to other owners, and a demand for either approval or a detailed written justification consistent with the recorded standards. Attaching photographs, copies of approvals granted to neighbors, and the relevant covenant excerpts strengthens your position. Threatening specific legal remedies—including a petition for declaratory judgment, injunctive relief, and recovery of attorney's fees if your covenants or the POA Act allow—signals that you are prepared to escalate. Many Georgia HOAs reverse improper denials at this stage to avoid litigation costs and the risk of an adverse ruling that could affect future enforcement. Send the letter via certified mail, return receipt requested, to both the HOA's registered agent (searchable through the Georgia Secretary of State) and the management company, preserving proof of delivery for any subsequent court filing.

Procedural Notes for Georgia

If your demand letter does not resolve the dispute, you can file suit in Georgia state court. Architectural disputes typically seek injunctive or declaratory relief, which generally cannot be pursued in magistrate (small claims) court—Georgia's $15,000 small claims limit applies to money damages only, not equitable relief. Most HOA architectural cases are filed in superior court, where filing fees range from approximately $200 to $300 depending on the county. Statutes of limitation vary: breach of written covenant claims generally carry a six-year limit under O.C.G.A. § 9-3-24. Some declarations require mediation or arbitration before litigation, so review your governing documents carefully. Prevailing-party attorney's fee provisions are common in Georgia HOA covenants and may be enforced under O.C.G.A. § 13-1-11.

Georgia HOA Law Overview

HOAs in Georgia are governed primarily by the Georgia Property Owners' Association Act (GPOAA) (O.C.G.A. §§ 44-3-220 through 44-3-235 (Title 44, Chapter 3, Article 6)). It sets the baseline rules for fines, records, meetings, and assessments that every association in the state must follow, regardless of what an individual board prefers.

There is no state agency that adjudicates Georgia HOA disputes. Historically: pursue the association's internal complaint process, then optional mediation/arbitration (e.g., Georgia Office of Dispute Resolution, AAA, or Georgia Academy of Mediators & Arbitrators), with a lawsuit in state court (Superior/State/Magistrate) as the last resort. Beginning 2027 under SB 406, associations must register with the Georgia Secretary of State and submit to a state complaint-and-hearing process. Knowing the exact statute and deadline before you write is what gives a demand letter its leverage.

A recent change to watch: Senate Bill 406 — the Georgia Property Owners' Bill of Rights Act — signed by Governor Kemp on May 12, 2026 (passed House 155-10 and Senate 51-0 on March 31, 2026). Most provisions effective January 1, 2027; attorney-fee provision effective July 1, 2026. Described as the most significant change to Georgia community-association law in decades.

Your Rights as a Georgia Homeowner

Meetings & notice: Meeting notice in Georgia: Governed primarily by the declaration/bylaws under the POAA; the Act does not set a single uniform statutory notice period for POA member meetings. Board and member meetings are generally open to owners. As permitted by the governing documents; the POAA leaves most meeting mechanics to the declaration/bylaws.

Fines & penalties: Georgia sets no statutory dollar cap on HOA fines — any limit comes from your recorded CC&Rs, and a court still tests whether a fine is reasonable and evenly enforced. No statewide statutory fine cap. Fines must be expressly authorized by the CC&Rs, and the association must follow whatever notice/hearing procedure its own governing documents require. The 10-day written notice in O.C.G.A. 44-3-223 applies specifically when the association seeks injunctive relief, not as a universal pre-fine hearing mandate. Late fees are statutorily capped at the greater of $10 or 10% of the unpaid amount, with interest up to 10%/yr (O.C.G.A. 44-3-232). Under SB 406, more detailed notice procedures before assessing fines take effect Jan 1, 2027, and payments must be applied to assessments before fines/fees.

Records access: As a Georgia homeowner you have a statutory right to inspect and copy association records. Response window: No specific statutory day-count in the current POAA; the association must maintain itemized financial records and meeting minutes (O.C.G.A. 44-3-231) available to members. SB 406 will add a 10-year retention requirement (including electronic records) effective 2027. No specific statutory monetary penalty in the current POAA; enforced by court action. SB 406 adds enforcement mechanisms effective 2027.

Resolving a dispute: In Georgia, mediation is available but not mandatory; small-claims court is available for smaller money disputes. No statewide mandatory mediation or arbitration. Voluntary mediation/arbitration available via the Georgia Office of Dispute Resolution, AAA, or the Georgia Academy of Mediators & Arbitrators. Magistrate (small claims) court available for small money disputes; larger matters go to State/Superior Court. SB 406 (effective 2027) introduces a state complaint-and-hearing process.

Common HOA Disputes in Georgia

  • Excessive or inconsistently applied fines, and selective (non-uniform) covenant enforcement
  • Uncertainty over whether the POAA even applies (opt-in) versus general covenant law, affecting owner rights
  • Assessment liens and foreclosure over unpaid dues/fines (tightened by SB 406)

Georgia Homeowner Protections Worth Knowing

  • OPT-IN structure: the GPOAA applies ONLY if the declaration expressly submits the association to O.C.G.A. §44-3-220 et seq.; associations existing when the Act passed did not automatically become subject to it, and non-opted-in communities are governed by general covenant/contract law
  • Statutory cap on late fees (greater of $10 or 10% of the unpaid amount) and interest (max 10%/yr) under O.C.G.A. 44-3-232
  • SB 406 Property Owners' Bill of Rights Act (2026): raises the foreclosure threshold and excludes fines/late fees/specific assessments from the amount that can trigger foreclosure — only regular assessments count
  • SB 406: payments must be applied to assessments before fines or other fees; 10-year records retention; Secretary-of-State registration; detailed pre-fine notice procedures (effective Jan 1, 2027)
  • SB 406 attorney-fee itemization and judicial review for reasonableness (effective July 1, 2026 for actions filed on/after that date)

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my Georgia HOA deny my architectural request for any reason?
No. Georgia courts require HOAs to exercise architectural review authority reasonably and in good faith. Denials must be based on standards in the recorded declaration and applied consistently. Arbitrary, capricious, or selectively enforced denials can be overturned in court. Under O.C.G.A. § 44-5-60, ambiguous covenants are construed against the HOA, meaning if the rule is unclear, the homeowner generally prevails.
What if my HOA never responded to my architectural request?
Many Georgia HOA covenants contain automatic approval clauses—if the ARC fails to respond within a specified period (often 30, 45, or 60 days), the request is deemed approved. Check your declaration carefully. If automatic approval applies, your demand letter should cite the specific provision, the date of submission, and demand written confirmation that the modification is approved as a matter of contract.
Does the Georgia Property Owners' Association Act apply to my HOA?
Only if your community has affirmatively submitted to it by recording language adopting the POA Act in the declaration. Many older Georgia HOAs are not POA Act communities and are governed solely by their covenants and Georgia common law. Check the first pages of your recorded declaration for language referencing O.C.G.A. § 44-3-220 et seq. to determine which framework applies.
Can I recover attorney's fees if I sue my HOA in Georgia?
Possibly. Many Georgia HOA declarations contain prevailing-party attorney's fee clauses that work both ways. Additionally, O.C.G.A. § 13-1-11 allows fee recovery on certain contract claims, and O.C.G.A. § 13-6-11 permits fees where the HOA acted in bad faith or caused unnecessary trouble and expense. A demand letter establishes a record that supports a later bad-faith fee claim if the HOA refuses to engage.
Should I file in small claims (magistrate) court for an architectural dispute?
Generally no. Georgia magistrate court has a $15,000 limit and primarily handles money damages, not injunctive or declaratory relief, which is typically what architectural disputes require. Most architectural denial cases belong in superior court, where judges can order the HOA to approve your modification, prevent enforcement actions, or interpret covenant language. Consult a Georgia HOA attorney to determine the proper venue.
Are there HOA fine limits in Georgia?
Georgia does not set a statutory dollar cap on HOA fines; the limit comes from your recorded CC&Rs, and fines must be reasonable and consistently enforced.
How long does an HOA have to respond to a records request in Georgia?
Yes — Georgia homeowners have a statutory right to inspect association records. Response window: No specific statutory day-count in the current POAA; the association must maintain itemized financial records and meeting minutes (O.C.G.A. 44-3-231) available to members. SB 406 will add a 10-year retention requirement (including electronic records) effective 2027. No specific statutory monetary penalty in the current POAA; enforced by court action. SB 406 adds enforcement mechanisms effective 2027.
Is there a state agency that regulates HOAs in Georgia?
No. Georgia has no state agency that adjudicates HOA disputes; homeowners enforce their rights through the courts. A statute-cited demand letter is the practical first step.
