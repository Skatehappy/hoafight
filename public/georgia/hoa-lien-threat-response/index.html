HOA Lien or Foreclosure Threat Response Law in Georgia

Georgia HOA liens are governed primarily by the Georgia Property Owners' Association Act (O.C.G.A. § 44-3-220 through § 44-3-235) for communities that have formally submitted to the Act, and by the Georgia Condominium Act (O.C.G.A. § 44-3-70 et seq.) for condominiums. For HOAs not submitted to the POAA, only contract law and the recorded covenants govern—meaning many associations have far less lien power than they claim. Under O.C.G.A. § 44-3-232, a properly submitted POAA association has an automatic statutory lien for unpaid assessments, late charges, interest (capped at 10% per year unless covenants specify otherwise), and reasonable collection costs including attorney's fees. However, the association must follow strict procedures: assessments must be properly levied at a duly noticed meeting, the owner must receive written demand, and any lien must be filed in the county Superior Court records. Foreclosure of an HOA lien in Georgia generally requires a judicial proceeding unless the covenants contain a valid power of sale—and even then, the association must comply with O.C.G.A. § 44-14-162 notice requirements, including publication and certified mail notice at least 30 days before sale. Owners have defenses including improper notice, miscalculated balances, fines that exceed authority granted in the declaration, charges accrued during periods the HOA failed to maintain common areas, and selective enforcement. Georgia also requires HOAs to provide a statement of account upon written request, and an owner can dispute the amount before the lien attaches or matures into foreclosure.

How a Demand Letter Works in Georgia

A Georgia HOA lien response letter works because it shifts the burden back to the association to prove every element of its claim. Start by demanding a full itemized accounting of all assessments, late fees, interest, and attorney's fees, citing your right under the POAA and the recorded declaration. Require the HOA to identify the specific recorded covenant or board resolution authorizing each charge. If you suspect the HOA has not formally submitted to the Property Owners' Association Act, demand proof of submission—without it, the association lacks statutory lien rights and must rely solely on contract remedies. Cite O.C.G.A. § 44-3-232 and § 44-3-109 for procedural requirements, and reference O.C.G.A. § 44-14-162 if foreclosure has been threatened, demanding strict compliance with notice and publication rules. Include a clear dispute of any improper fines, requesting copies of hearing notices and board minutes that imposed them. State that you reserve all rights under Georgia law including claims for wrongful lien (O.C.G.A. § 44-14-380), slander of title, and violations of the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act if a third-party collector or attorney is involved. Set a firm 30-day deadline to respond, withdraw improper charges, or release the lien. A documented letter creates a paper trail that judges and juries find persuasive, often prompts the HOA's attorney to negotiate, and preserves your defenses if the matter proceeds to Superior Court or a foreclosure action.

Procedural Notes for Georgia

HOA lien disputes in Georgia are typically heard in the Superior Court of the county where the property is located because they involve real property and equitable relief. Pure money disputes under $15,000 may be filed in Magistrate (small claims) Court, but liens and foreclosure cannot be removed there. Filing fees in Superior Court generally range from $200 to $230. Georgia has no specific statute of limitations for HOA assessment liens beyond the general six-year contract limitation under O.C.G.A. § 9-3-24, but liens themselves expire four years after the assessment becomes due unless the HOA files suit (O.C.G.A. § 44-3-232(d)). Always send your demand letter by certified mail, return receipt requested, and keep proof.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my Georgia HOA really foreclose on my home over unpaid dues?
Yes, but only under specific conditions. If your community is properly submitted to the Property Owners' Association Act and the covenants grant a power of sale, the HOA may pursue non-judicial foreclosure following O.C.G.A. § 44-14-162 notice rules. Otherwise, the HOA must file a judicial foreclosure action in Superior Court. Many associations threaten foreclosure they cannot legally execute, which is why a demand letter requiring proof of authority is often effective at stopping aggressive collection.
What if my HOA never submitted to the Property Owners' Association Act?
Many older Georgia HOAs were never formally submitted to the POAA. Submission requires a recorded declaration that specifically invokes O.C.G.A. § 44-3-220 et seq. Without submission, the HOA cannot claim the automatic statutory lien rights, expanded fee recovery, or other POAA protections—it must rely solely on contract terms in the recorded covenants. Demanding proof of submission is one of the most powerful early steps in disputing a Georgia HOA lien.
Can I dispute fines that the HOA added to my lien?
Yes. Under Georgia law, fines must be authorized by the recorded declaration, properly noticed, and imposed only after the owner receives notice and an opportunity to be heard. If the HOA cannot produce hearing notices, board minutes, or a covenant provision authorizing the fine amount, those charges can be challenged and removed. Improper fines lumped into a lien can sometimes invalidate the entire lien if not properly itemized.
How long do I have to respond to an HOA lien notice in Georgia?
While Georgia law does not set a single universal response deadline, most HOA pre-lien notices give owners 30 days to cure or dispute the balance. If foreclosure is threatened, you must receive at least 30 days notice before any sale under O.C.G.A. § 44-14-162. Acting quickly—ideally within 30 days—preserves your defenses, prevents additional fees, and maximizes leverage before the HOA's attorney files suit or records additional documents.
Can I recover attorney's fees if the HOA's lien is wrongful?
Possibly. Under O.C.G.A. § 44-14-380 and related wrongful lien provisions, an owner who proves the HOA filed a false or improper lien may recover damages, including attorney's fees. Additionally, if the covenants provide for prevailing-party attorney's fees, that clause typically works both ways. Claims for slander of title and bad-faith litigation under O.C.G.A. § 13-6-11 may also support fee recovery if the HOA acted unreasonably or refused to correct a documented error.
