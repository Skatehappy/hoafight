HOA Architectural Approval Denial Law in Pennsylvania

Pennsylvania regulates planned communities through the Uniform Planned Community Act (UPCA), 68 Pa.C.S. § 5101 et seq., enacted in 1996, and condominiums through the Uniform Condominium Act, 68 Pa.C.S. § 3101 et seq. Both statutes impose duties of good faith on associations and their boards. Under 68 Pa.C.S. § 5302, associations must exercise their powers reasonably and consistent with the declaration, bylaws, and rules. Section 5303 imposes a duty of good faith on every contract or duty governed by the Act. When an architectural review committee denies a homeowner's application, Pennsylvania courts apply the "reasonableness" standard. The denial must be based on objective criteria contained in the declaration or rules properly adopted under § 5302. An HOA cannot create new restrictions on the fly, deny based on personal preference, or apply standards inconsistently to similarly situated homeowners. Pennsylvania courts have repeatedly held that restrictive covenants are strictly construed against the party seeking enforcement (the HOA), with ambiguities resolved in favor of the free use of property. See Vernon Township Volunteer Fire Department v. Connor and similar cases. Pennsylvania also requires procedural fairness: under § 5308, owners are entitled to notice and an opportunity to be heard before adverse action. Associations must keep records and provide them to owners under § 5308.1. Federal and state law also override certain HOA restrictions—solar panels are protected under reasonable use principles, satellite dishes under FCC OTARD rules, and flag display under federal law. If the association violates the UPCA or its own documents, § 5412 authorizes a court to award reasonable attorney's fees and costs to the prevailing homeowner, making demand letters a powerful tool.

How a Demand Letter Works in Pennsylvania

An effective Pennsylvania HOA demand letter accomplishes several goals at once. First, it documents the dispute in writing, creating a paper trail that courts and judges take seriously. Second, it forces the association's board and attorney to evaluate their legal exposure under 68 Pa.C.S. § 5412, which permits attorney-fee awards to prevailing homeowners. Boards often reverse course when they realize a denial could cost the association thousands in legal fees. Your demand letter should identify the specific provision of the declaration or architectural guidelines you complied with, attach your application materials, point out any inconsistencies in how the HOA has treated similar requests from other owners, and cite the UPCA's good-faith and reasonableness requirements. The letter should request a specific remedy: written approval of your project, a formal hearing under § 5308, or production of the records the board relied on. Set a firm deadline—typically 14 to 30 days—and warn that you will pursue legal action, including a claim for declaratory judgment, injunctive relief, damages, and attorney's fees, if the association does not comply. Many Pennsylvania HOAs are managed by professional management companies that immediately escalate demand letters to legal counsel, who then advise the board to settle. Sending the letter by certified mail with return receipt to the board president, the management company, and the association's registered agent maximizes pressure and preserves proof of delivery for any later court filing.

Procedural Notes for Pennsylvania

Pennsylvania's Magisterial District Courts handle small claims up to $12,000, which is appropriate for damages claims like fines wrongfully imposed or costs incurred from a delayed project. Filing fees range from approximately $80 to $180 depending on claim size. For declaratory judgment or injunctive relief ordering the HOA to approve your modification, you must file in the Court of Common Pleas in the county where the property is located, where filing fees typically run $200 to $400. The statute of limitations for breach of restrictive covenants and contract claims is four years under 42 Pa.C.S. § 5525. Some declarations require mediation or alternative dispute resolution before litigation—review your governing documents carefully. Pennsylvania does not have a mandatory pre-suit notice statute for HOA disputes, but sending a demand letter is strongly recommended.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my Pennsylvania HOA deny my architectural request for any reason?
No. Under 68 Pa.C.S. § 5302 and § 5303, your HOA must act reasonably and in good faith. Denials must be based on standards actually written in the declaration, bylaws, or properly adopted rules. The board cannot deny your project based on personal taste, undisclosed criteria, or standards applied inconsistently to other homeowners. Pennsylvania courts strictly construe restrictive covenants against the HOA, meaning ambiguities favor your right to use your property. Arbitrary or capricious denials are unenforceable and can result in court-ordered approval plus attorney's fees.
How long does my HOA have to respond to my architectural application in Pennsylvania?
Pennsylvania's UPCA does not set a universal deadline, but your declaration or architectural guidelines almost always specify a response time—commonly 30, 45, or 60 days. Many declarations include automatic approval clauses: if the architectural committee fails to respond within the stated period, the application is deemed approved. Review your governing documents carefully. If the deadline has passed without a written denial, you may have a strong argument that approval is automatic, and a demand letter citing this provision often resolves the dispute quickly.
Can I recover attorney's fees if I sue my Pennsylvania HOA?
Yes, in many cases. Under 68 Pa.C.S. § 5412 of the Uniform Planned Community Act, a court may award reasonable attorney's fees and costs to the prevailing party in actions to enforce the Act, the declaration, bylaws, or rules. This fee-shifting provision makes Pennsylvania more homeowner-friendly than many states. The possibility of paying your legal fees often motivates HOA boards to settle after receiving a demand letter, since defending an indefensible denial could cost the association tens of thousands of dollars.
Does Pennsylvania protect solar panels from HOA denials?
Pennsylvania does not have a comprehensive solar access statute as strong as those in California or Florida, but HOA solar restrictions still must be reasonable under the UPCA's good-faith standard. Blanket bans on solar panels are vulnerable to challenge as unreasonable, especially where the declaration is silent or ambiguous. Some HOAs may regulate placement and appearance but cannot effectively prohibit solar energy systems. A demand letter emphasizing reasonableness, energy policy, and the UPCA's good-faith requirement can succeed even without a dedicated solar rights statute.
What court do I file in if my Pennsylvania HOA refuses to back down?
It depends on the relief you want. For monetary damages up to $12,000—such as reimbursement for fines, increased construction costs, or lost rental income—file in your local Magisterial District Court (small claims). For an order forcing the HOA to approve your project (injunctive relief) or a declaration of your rights, file in the Court of Common Pleas in the county where your property sits. Court of Common Pleas cases are more complex and usually require an attorney, but the UPCA's attorney-fee provision can offset that cost if you prevail.
